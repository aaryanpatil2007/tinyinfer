The history of computing is usually told as a story about machines, but it is better understood as a story about abstraction. Every generation of hardware exposed a surface that the generation of software above it learned to treat as solid ground, and every generation of programmers eventually forgot that the ground was once somebody else's ceiling. The engineers who wired vacuum tubes into logic gates thought of a flip-flop as a fragile accomplishment; the programmers who came after thought of a bit as a fact of nature. This forgetting is not a failure. It is the entire mechanism by which the field moves. A profession that had to remember everything about the layers beneath it would never have the spare attention to build anything new on top.

Consider what happens when a modern program adds two numbers. The source code expresses an intention in a notation designed for human reading. A compiler translates that intention into an intermediate form, reasons about it, discards the parts that do not matter, and emits instructions for a processor that will itself quietly rewrite them, reorder them, and execute several of them at once while pretending, for the benefit of anyone watching, that it did no such thing. The pretense is load-bearing. Software correctness rests on the processor's promise that all of its internal cleverness is invisible, that the observable result is exactly what a naive machine would have produced, only sooner. Nearly every performance technique in hardware is a variation on this single idea: do something risky and fast in private, and keep a careful public story in which nothing unusual ever happened.

Memory is where the story gets expensive. Arithmetic became fast much more quickly than data became close, and the gap between the two has shaped forty years of computer architecture. A processor can multiply numbers hundreds of times faster than it can fetch them from main memory, which means that most of what a fast computer does all day is wait. The caches, the prefetchers, the reorder buffers, the branch predictors: all of it exists to hide the waiting, to keep the arithmetic units fed, to maintain the illusion that memory is near. When people who optimize programs for a living say that modern performance work is really memory work, this is what they mean. Counting the operations a program performs tells you almost nothing about how long it will take. Counting the bytes it must move, and noticing how far those bytes must travel, tells you almost everything.

The arrival of large neural networks made this old truth newly vivid. A language model is, mechanically, an enormous quantity of numbers that must be dragged past a comparatively small amount of arithmetic, over and over, once for every token it produces. When such a model generates text one token at a time, the processor spends almost all of its effort reloading the same billions of weights it loaded a moment ago, performing a brief flurry of multiplication, and then reloading them again. The arithmetic is nearly free; the procession of bytes is the whole cost. Every meaningful technique for making these models respond faster is, at bottom, a scheme for moving fewer bytes or for moving them fewer times: remembering yesterday's intermediate results instead of recomputing them, storing each weight in fewer bits than caution would suggest, fusing adjacent steps so their intermediate products never make the round trip to memory at all.

There is a pleasing symmetry in the fact that the cheapest of these techniques is simply remembering. A model that generates a paragraph recomputes, at each new word, quantities it already computed for every previous word, and the values have not changed, because the past does not change. Caching them is not an optimization so much as the retraction of a mistake. The deeper techniques require consent to a small amount of damage. Stored in fewer bits, every weight in the network becomes slightly wrong, and the practitioner must measure whether millions of small wrongs add up to a visible difference in behavior or dissolve into the noise the network never noticed anyway. The discipline lies in the measuring. An engineer who claims a speedup without a quality number has reported half of a result, and the less flattering half is the one left out.

What makes this corner of engineering satisfying is that the feedback is honest. The program emits the same words per second whether or not its author slept well, and a benchmark run on the same machine with the same prompt either improves on yesterday's number or it does not. There are few fields left where a practitioner can hold the entire tower of abstractions in mind at once, from the notation at the top to the procession of bytes at the bottom, and watch a single idea travel the whole distance down and come back up as a number. The number is small and specific and does not care about anyone's feelings. That is precisely its charm.
